The U.S. baby name market

Every year, the Social Security Administration publishes the name of nearly every baby born in America. Treat those names like stocks — with parents as the traders — and you get a 145-year market history: blue chips, hot IPOs, slow collapses, and the occasional meme-stock frenzy. §1 · Market fundamentals

How big is the market, and who’s in it?

In 2024, 3.61 million babies received Social Security numbers — 51.1% boys, 48.9% girls. That’s the total float of the name market, and it has been shrinking: the all-time peak was 4.33 million in 2007.

The chart below is the market’s trading volume since 1880. The early decades undercount badly — Social Security didn’t exist until 1935, so people born in the 1880s only appear if they later applied for a card. The Baby Boom is the huge swell from 1946; the 2008 recession starts the long modern slide.

Go deeper: why more boys?

The human sex ratio at birth is typically around 105 boys for every 100 girls, which works out to about:

  • 51.2% male
  • 48.8% female

This has been observed across populations for centuries, although it varies a bit by country and over time. The natural follow-up is: why?

The leading explanation is that it’s an evolutionary compensation for the fact that males have higher mortality throughout life, especially early in life. Historically, boys were more likely than girls to:

  • die in infancy,
  • die from childhood diseases,
  • die from accidents,
  • and later die from violence, warfare, and risky behavior.

By producing a slight surplus of males at birth, the number of men and women who survive to reproductive age ends up closer to equal. This idea traces back to the statistician Ronald Fisher and what’s now called Fisher’s principle. The basic argument is elegant:

  • If one sex became too rare, individuals producing that sex would have more grandchildren on average.
  • Natural selection would therefore favor genes producing the rarer sex.
  • The system stabilizes near parity.

That explains why it’s close to 50:50. The persistent 105:100 bias is thought to reflect the extra mortality faced by males before reproduction.

Why are boys more fragile?

Male fetuses and infants are, on average, biologically more vulnerable. Compared with females, they have higher rates of miscarriage later in pregnancy, stillbirth, premature birth, birth complications, and infant mortality. Part of this appears to be genetic:

  • Females have two X chromosomes, providing a backup copy for many genes.
  • Males have only one X chromosome, so harmful recessive mutations are more likely to have effects.
  • Male fetal development also appears somewhat more sensitive to maternal stress and adverse conditions.

In other words, nature starts with a few extra boys because more boys are lost before adulthood.

Is it always 105:100?

Not exactly. Typical natural populations fall between about 103 and 107 boys per 100 girls. The ratio can be nudged slightly by parental age, environmental stress, severe famines, and some environmental toxins. After major disasters or prolonged stress, researchers have sometimes observed a small dip in the proportion of male births, though the effect is modest and not universal.

Go deeper: how we quote a name’s price

Raw counts mislead across eras — there were twice as many babies in 1957 as in 1935. So every trend on this page uses a name’s rate per million births of that sex. In 2024, 14,718 girls were named Olivia out of 1,613,188 named girls: a rate of 9,124 per million, or about 0.9%.

For long windows we compare three-year averages (1994–96 vs. 2022–24) so a single odd year can’t fake a trend.

§2 · The big board

Biggest gainers and losers

Over 30 years the leaderboard has completely turned over. Liam — a niche pick in 1995 — gained more market share than any name in the country. Michael lost the most of any name on record: 2.2% of all boys in 1995, under 0.5% today.

A falling name is still a big name — 8,189 boys were named Michael in 2024, more than all but a dozen names. But in this market, yesterday’s index fund is tomorrow’s “dad name.”

§7 · The unisex desk

Names traded on both sides

The SSA records every baby as M or F — there is no unisex or non-binary category in this data — and counts each name separately per sex, so we measure instead of label: a name is shared in a given year when at least 20% of its babies are the minority sex. By that bar, almost no American babies had a shared name for over a century — the share hovered near 2% from 1880 to 1990. It has tripled since, hitting an all-time high of 6.0% in 2024, when just 44 names with 500 or more babies managed even a 30–70 split. Clerical artifacts and genuine exceptions

Browse mid-century SSA data and you’ll find a few hundred girl Johns, boy Jessicas, and boy Marys in almost every year. Most are not naming trends—they’re data artifacts.

Until the late 1980s, Social Security numbers were issued from handwritten applications keyed by clerks, and about 0.3% of records had the sex field entered incorrectly. Applied to 90,000+ annual boy Johns, that error rate alone produces a few hundred phantom girl Johns. The signature is unmistakable: recorded girl Johns rise and fall almost perfectly in proportion to boy Johns, at roughly 1:300.

That relationship largely disappears after Enumeration at Birth, introduced beginning in 1987, when Social Security numbers began being assigned directly from hospital birth certificates. Recorded girl Johns fall from 233 in 1985 to just 8 in 2024.

The line never reaches zero, though, because a small number of these records are genuine. Family surnames become given names, traditions like Johnnie persist, and occasional examples—such as actress Michael Learned—have always existed. Enumeration at Birth removed the clerical artifacts; it revealed the underlying baseline.

The 20% shared-name threshold used in this analysis sits comfortably above both the historical error rate and the small population of genuinely cross-sex names.

§8 · The long view

The great fragmentation

The biggest change in this market is concentration. In 1950, a third of American boys had a top-10 name; in 2024 it was 8%, and the girls’ market is even more dispersed.

Economists would call it de-concentration. In 1950 the girls’ market behaved like it had roughly 100 names in circulation; by 2024 it behaved like 651. Parents stopped buying the index and started stock-picking — which is exactly why the spikes and crashes above have gotten sharper: with no giant incumbents, a small name can moon in a season.

Go deeper: what “effective number of names” means

It’s the inverse of the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index, the same concentration measure antitrust regulators use. If every baby had one of exactly N equally common names, the index would read N. It answers: “the market behaves as if how many names are competing?”

§9 · Prospectus & fine print

How this data works

Source

The SSA’s public baby-names files: national counts from 1880, state counts from 1910, updated each May. This page uses the 2024 release. ssa.gov/oact/babynames. Name origins draw on Wiktionary (CC BY-SA 4.0), plus NAMEDAQ spelling-matches and desk notes.

The 5-baby rule

Names given to fewer than 5 babies of a sex in a year are suppressed for privacy. That’s why “named” totals (3.33M in 2024) run below true births (3.61M) — the gap is thousands of ultra-rare names.

Spelling is destiny

Every spelling is its own listing. Aiden, Ayden, Aidan, Aden, Aydin and Aaden all charted separately in the 2000s — combined, the Aiden complex was briefly a top-3 “name.”

Early data is thin

Social Security began in 1935; earlier birth years only include people who survived long enough to get a card. Volumes are undercounts, and the sex mix reflects who enrolled, not who was born: 1880s cohorts skew male (numbers came through employment), 1890–1930 cohorts skew heavily female (longer-lived survivors enrolling via 1960s benefit programs). From ~1940 on, the data shows true births — about 105 boys per 100 girls.
